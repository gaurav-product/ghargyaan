# 02 · Research

> **Research required.** No primary user research has been run yet. Nothing in this file reports user findings. It lists what we believe, why, and how we will find out.

## Desk research done for V1
| Area | What we did | What we learned |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical content | Read NHS, WHO, NIH (NCCIH), MedlinePlus, AAP, AAD, AAO, FDA, EPA and UK FSA pages for each guide. Every cited URL was checked to load (HTTP 200) on 2026-09-26 and the cited claim was matched against the page text. | Public-health guidance for minor first aid and common discomfort is consistent and well sourced. Evidence for most traditional remedies is thin, mixed, or absent. |
| Safety | Mapped emergency signs from NHS pages (burns, head injury, poisoning, anaphylaxis, stroke, dehydration, bites). | A small set of red flags covers most "do not self-treat" cases. |
| Indian context | Verified India helplines: 112, 108, AIIMS Poisons Information Centre 1800 116 117, Tele-MANAS 14416, LPG emergency 1906. | Emergency numbers are simple; poison and mental-health lines are less known. |
| Traditional practice | Collected 46 practices commonly used in Indian homes (haldi, kadha, bhaap, ghutti, kajal, tel in ear, isabgol, copper water…). | Several are actively discouraged by public-health guidance (honey under 1, bowl steam for children, kohl with lead, oil in the ear, butter/toothpaste on burns). |

## Assumptions to test
1. People search first, then ask family, then (maybe) a pharmacist. **Unknown order.**
2. Hinglish is the dominant typed form for urban 20–40 year olds. **Hypothesis.**
3. An evidence label increases trust rather than reducing it for traditional remedies. **Hypothesis; risk of backlash.**
4. Parents are the highest-intent segment (child illness triggers searching). **Hypothesis.**
5. Voice matters more for older users and Hindi-first users. **Hypothesis.**

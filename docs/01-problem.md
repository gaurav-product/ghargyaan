# 01 · Problem

## The everyday moment
A child gets a mosquito bite. Someone burns a finger on a hot tawa. A throat has been scratchy for two days. In most Indian homes, the first answer comes from a parent, a grandparent, a WhatsApp forward, a YouTube video or a search result, not from a doctor. That is reasonable: most of these situations are minor and manageable at home.

The trouble is that the advice arriving in that moment is **mixed**:

- Some of it is excellent and evidence-backed (cool running water on a burn, honey for a cough in children over one).
- Some of it is harmless tradition with little evidence (haldi doodh for a cold).
- Some of it is harmful (toothpaste or ghee on a burn, honey for a newborn, oil in an aching ear, kajal on a baby's eyes).
- And occasionally, the "minor" situation is actually an emergency that needs help now (a dog bite, a burn on the face, a baby with fever).

People have no easy way to tell these apart in the moment.

## Problem statement
> Indian families handling everyday health and household situations get fragmented advice that mixes useful traditional knowledge, unsupported claims and unsafe practices, with no signal about which is which and weak signals about when to stop self-caring and get help.

## Why now
- Smartphone and data access are widespread; voice input in Hindi and Indian English is now practical in browsers.
- Language models make natural, Hinglish-tolerant input feasible, but they also make confident-sounding, ungrounded health answers cheap. That raises the stakes on safety and grounding.

## What is *not* the problem
- Diagnosing disease. GharGyaan deliberately does not do this.
- Replacing doctors, pharmacies or telemedicine.
- Access to care for serious illness.

## Status
**Hypothesis.** The framing above comes from desk research and product intuition. It has not yet been validated with users. See [15-validation.md](15-validation.md).
